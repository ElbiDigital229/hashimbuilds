Rebuilding The Product Without Stopping The Business.

How I reconstructed a decade of undocumented marketplace logic, moved the product from a directory to a sourcing workflow, and put AI only where it was allowed to be wrong.

Overview

A thirteen-year-old marketplace connecting brands with US manufacturers.

Both sides of the market were real and still trading. The product underneath them had accumulated a decade of patches, exceptions, one-off fixes and manual workarounds — and somewhere in that decade the company had stopped being able to change it safely.

I came in fractionally, as product owner. What I owned: reconstructing how the product actually worked, deciding what to build next, and getting it built against a marketplace that could not be switched off.

The Business Was Stuck

It didn't need more features. It had lost the ability to change itself.

Small product changes required disproportionate engineering involvement, because nobody could predict what else they touched. Operations absorbed the gaps by hand. New buyers weren't reaching supplier conversations; factories complained that the enquiries they did get were too vague to answer.

Every symptom looked like a feature request. None of them were.

Product Forensics

Before I could define the next product, I had to reconstruct the current one.

I treated the first month as archaeology rather than design: stakeholder and customer interviews, support conversations read in bulk, operations shadowed through onboarding and verification, the highest-volume journeys traced with engineering, and the manual processes that lived entirely outside the product written down for the first time.

What was hiding in there

  • Factory category labels looked like metadata. They also controlled search eligibility.
  • Certain subscriptions silently changed how suppliers were ranked.
  • Deleted supplier records couldn't actually disappear — historical buyer conversations depended on them.
  • Some onboarding questions existed to serve internal process, not the customer.
  • Changing one part of onboarding could break lead handling with no visible link between them.

The output was the first shared source of truth: users, jobs, journeys, rules, permissions, data, monetisation, exceptions.

The Real Problem

The assumption was that buyers needed better search. They didn't.

Many buyers didn't know what to search for. A founder arrives thinking “I need someone to manufacture a premium stainless-steel bottle.” A manufacturer needs material, capacity, tolerances, quantity, process, finish, certifications, packaging, target price, lead time and geography before they can even quote.

So buyers submitted incomplete briefs, factories deprioritised vague enquiries, and buyers read the silence as a bad marketplace. The directory wasn't failing. It was answering a question buyers couldn't yet ask.

The marketplace was built around searching a directory. The actual job was turning an idea into a manufacturable project.

Competing Stakeholders

Five groups, five different products.

Founder

An autonomous AI agent that finds manufacturers for customers.

Sales

Enterprise accounts, team permissions, features that close larger deals.

Operations

Fewer low-quality enquiries and less manual lead cleanup.

Engineering

A full platform rewrite before anything new is added.

Customers

Relevant suppliers, and an actual reply.

My job

Turn five legitimate positions into one thing the team could ship.

The Decision

One vertical slice, not four programmes.

Every request was scored against customer impact, revenue, marketplace liquidity, effort, technical risk, operational burden, reversibility and strategic leverage. Four of the five asks lost — and the reasons matter more than the outcome.

Not an autonomous agent

Too broad to control, and it would have been reasoning over supplier data we already knew was unreliable.

Not more filters

Filters assume the buyer knows the vocabulary. That assumption was the problem.

Not a rewrite first

Twelve months of invisible work the business could not afford to wait through.

Brief → match → conversation. The marketplace's core transaction, and nothing else.

Where AI Belonged

The important decision was what AI wasn't allowed to touch.

Interpretation is a good use of a probabilistic system: reading a sentence, extracting a requirement, noticing an omission. Entitlement is not. Pricing, permissions, compliance and eligibility have to be predictable and auditable, and a model that is right most of the time is the wrong tool for a question that has to be right every time.

AI for interpretation and recommendation. Deterministic software for anything that moves money or grants access.

How we knew it was good enough to ship

Plausible text is not a passing grade. The brief extractor had to clear product-level thresholds before it was allowed in front of a buyer.

The first version of the interaction failed

The logic was sound and the experience was not. Asking clarification questions in sequence meant some buyers faced ten or twelve of them before seeing anything useful. Completion fell.

AI should reduce work before it asks the user to do more work.

How Matching Worked

Four stages, and only one of them is a model.

Letting a language model choose which factories to recommend would have been simpler to build and impossible to defend. Instead, eligibility is decided by rules, relevance is decided by comparison, and every recommendation arrives with its reason attached.

The supply side had to be fixed first

Thousands of supplier profiles said things like “we provide high-quality manufacturing services.” That is not an input a matching system can use. An enrichment pipeline turned years of unstructured material — profile text, documents, websites, past projects, operations notes — into structured capability data, with a confidence score, and anything low-confidence routed to a human rather than published.

The visible feature was matching. The work that made it possible was on the supply side, where nobody was looking.

A New Problem

Matching improved, and factories started complaining.

They were receiving more enquiries, and not all of them were worth answering. A project could be technically relevant and still be too early, too vague, or commercially unrealistic. We had solved supplier relevance and left lead readiness untouched.

A readiness score — brief completeness, budget, quantity, timeline, buyer verification — let factories choose their own floor rather than receive everything. Buyers kept the freedom to explore; factories stopped paying for it.

Getting the right supplier in front of a buyer isn't enough. The opportunity has to deserve their attention.

Rebuilding Live

Neither option on the table was acceptable.

Patch the old platform indefinitely, or freeze product development for a year while engineering rebuilt it. I took neither, and worked with engineering on a strangler migration instead: the new system took the journey we were changing, the legacy platform kept everything we weren't, and the boundary moved a phase at a time.

This wasn't a redesign. It was a live-platform replacement with the shop still open.

From Strategy to Delivery

A product decision only counts once someone can build and test it.

The reframe was the interesting part. Getting it into a sprint was the part that determined whether any of it happened.

Worked example — AI-assisted brief creation

As a buyer who may not understand manufacturing terminology, I want to describe my product naturally, so that MakersRow can turn my idea into a supplier-ready brief.

Acceptance criteria

  1. Known requirements are extracted from free-form input.
  2. Unknown values are never invented.
  3. Critical missing fields trigger clarification.
  4. AI-generated fields remain editable.
  5. Low-confidence fields are flagged.
  6. The buyer can continue without accepting any AI suggestion.
  7. The original input remains accessible.
  8. AI failure never blocks manual brief creation.
  9. Structured output maps to the same schema supplier matching reads.
  10. The buyer reviews the complete brief before it is submitted.

Half of those criteria exist to make the feature degrade safely. That ratio is the difference between shipping AI and demoing it.

The Billing Truth

The same problem, one layer down: the company could not tell who was paying it.

The application reported 908 active subscriptions. Stripe showed 282 customers who actually moved money in 2026.

Reconciling application state against Stripe showed that the product's definition of “active subscriber” had drifted away from commercial reality — and every dashboard, report and entitlement check in the business was reading the drifted number.

It is the same failure as the 137 rules, expressed in dollars: no single source of truth, and no way to know which answer was the real one.

Reflection

What I learned

A decade of undocumented decisions is a product, not a mess.

Every strange rule existed because something once required it. Reconstructing the reasons was more valuable than removing the rules, because it told us which ones we were allowed to change.

Solving one side of a marketplace creates a problem on the other.

Better matching produced worse leads. That isn't a bug in the matching — it's what happens when you optimise one half of a two-sided system and measure only that half.

The number a business reports about itself is a product decision.

908 versus 282 wasn't a data-quality issue. It was a schema that made the wrong answer the easy one to read, and it had been shaping decisions for years.

MakersRow didn't need an AI feature. It needed its product rebuilt so that AI could be useful.
