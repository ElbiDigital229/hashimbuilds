Fifteen Group Chats. Ten Thousand Assets. One System.

A coworking operator running fifteen buildings on WhatsApp — and a ticketing system that only started working once it stopped asking people what was urgent.

The Problem

Fifteen buildings coordinated entirely through group chat.

Daftarkhwan runs coworking space across Pakistan: roughly fifteen branches, each a full building hosting three to four hundred members, and more than five thousand active members in total. Its own team is around a hundred people — community managers, operations managers, and a facilities wing with its own head and senior staff.

Coordination happened in WhatsApp. A group per branch, separate groups for operations, fourteen or fifteen threads that a manager was expected to hold in their head at once. Underneath them sat well over ten thousand moving assets — air conditioners, chairs, internet links, generators, UPS units, lighting — any of which could be failing at any moment.

Some branches ran this well. Some did not. Nobody could tell you which, because the record of what had broken and who had fixed it was scattered across chat threads, a captain’s book and a few Google Sheets.

The information existed. It was just never in the same place twice.

Discovery

Every fix was reactive, and reactive fixes cost more.

Because faults were reported conversationally, nothing accumulated. A generator that had been patched four times looked exactly like a generator being patched for the first time. Servicing slipped, small faults were left until they became failures, and the failures were expensive.

The cost did not stop at maintenance. A member whose room is too hot, whose internet drops, whose lift is out, experiences that as the product. Unresolved faults fed directly into churn, and churn into revenue.

What operations actually needed to see

  • Everything in one queue, across all branches, instead of fifteen parallel conversations.
  • A real distinction between P1 and P4, so that a failed generator outranked a scuffed wall.
  • Which branches were performing and which were not — measured, not anecdotal.
  • Which of their own people were clearing work, and which were not.
  • A history per asset, so that repeated failure became visible as a pattern rather than a series of unrelated messages.

They did not need a chat replacement. They needed the same conversations to leave a record behind.

What Shipped First

Snap it, describe it, send it.

The first version did the obvious thing. A member or staff member sees a problem, opens the app, takes a photo, writes a description, categorises it, and the ticket routes to the relevant department. Everything landed in one queue with a status, an owner and an activity trail. Mobile apps went out so that the people who actually walk the buildings were not tied to a desk.

It worked, in the sense that tickets arrived and got closed. It took a few weeks of real use to see that the parts we had asked people to fill in were the parts that broke.

Where It Broke

Three fields, each one quietly useless.

Everything arrived as P1

Reporters set their own priority, so almost every ticket came in as priority one. Operations opened a queue in which nothing was more urgent than anything else, which is the same as having no priority at all.

Assignment added a hop nobody was doing work in

Tickets went to the operations team, who read them and passed them to whoever should actually handle it. On a team that size, that meant a ticket was reported, opened, read, reassigned, opened again, and only then acted on. Two of those five steps were people forwarding work rather than doing it.

The form was being defeated, not filled

We had asked for a title, a description, an asset and a due date, because we wanted the record. In practice people wrote everything into the title, left the description empty, and set every due date to today. The form took longer and captured less.

Every one of those was a rational response to the form we shipped. People were not misusing it. They were routing around it.

Let The Asset Decide

Stop asking the reporter for anything they have no reason to answer honestly.

We removed the priority field. We removed the assignee field. We collapsed title and description into a single box: describe the problem. Then we asked for the one thing a reporter genuinely knows and has no incentive to distort — which thing is broken, and where.

Priority comes from the asset

I sat with the operations team and we went through the register. Is a failed air conditioner a P1 or a P2? A generator? A lift? Once the mapping existed, priority stopped being a claim and became a property of the equipment.

Routing comes from the asset

The same tag drives department. Generator faults reach the genset team; cleanliness reaches housekeeping; HVAC reaches HVAC. The ticket arrives with the right people already on it.

The rules belong to the client, not to us

All of it is editable in an admin panel: if the asset matches this, assign that department, at that priority. This was the requirement I cared most about. A routing system that needs its builder every time the organisation changes shape is a dependency, not a tool.

The reporter’s job shrank to two things they cannot get wrong: what is broken, and what it looks like.

The Blocker System

A low completion rate is not the same as a person not working.

Once performance was visible, the reports started producing unfair answers. An engineer sitting at thirty per cent completion looked like the problem. He was not. He had inspected his tickets, priced the fix, and been waiting on finance to release funds that never came.

So assignees can mark a ticket blocked and tag the department or person holding it up. The ticket stays open, the clock keeps running, and the delay attaches to whoever caused it. A branch with poor numbers can now show that its numbers are poor for a reason that sits outside the branch.

A delayed ticket is fine if the system can say who is holding it and why. An untracked delay is the actual failure.

Where AI Belonged

First nothing, then titles, then the thing worth having.

The first version had no AI in it at all. Priority derivation, routing rules and the blocker system are ordinary logic, and they carried most of the benefit. That order was deliberate: anything a rule can do reliably should not be handed to a model.

The first genuine use was small. Because we had collapsed the form to one field, tickets had good descriptions and no titles — so a model writes the title from the description. The reporter gets one box; the queue still reads cleanly.

The one that changed conversations came later, once two to three months of tickets had accumulated against real assets. The system could finally say: this air conditioner has been repaired four times in two months and has cost you a hundred and ten thousand rupees. Projected across a year, continuing to repair it costs roughly twice what replacing it would — and replacement comes with a warranty.

That recommendation was never a modelling problem. It was a data-capture problem, solved eight months earlier by making people tag the asset.

  • Priority became meaningful, because it stopped being self-declared.
  • Performance became fair to read, because blocked time attaches to the blocker.
  • Maintenance moved from reactive to planned, with recurring schedules and preventive checklists generated on cadence.
  • Repair-versus-replace became a costed decision instead of a judgement call.
  • The client owns its own routing and changes it without coming back to us.

The platform is live at ticket.truenorthpk.com, with an Android app wrapping the same system and push notifications to the people who walk the buildings.

Built as a single TypeScript codebase — React and Vite on the front, Express and Prisma against PostgreSQL behind it — running twenty-two server modules, thirty-three screens, sixty permission keys and nineteen notification event types, deployed on a self-hosted runner behind nginx.

What I’d Carry Forward

Four things this project taught me properly.

Never ask a user to declare something they benefit from exaggerating.

Self-declared priority is not a data-quality problem you can train out of people. It is a design error, and the fix is to derive the field from something the reporter has no stake in.

A form people defeat is a form that asked the wrong questions.

Everything dumped in the title and every due date set to today told us precisely what was worth capturing and what was not. That behaviour was the research.

Measure the delay, not just the person.

Any completion metric without a blocking model will eventually punish the wrong people, and it will do it confidently.

Earn the AI by capturing the data first.

The valuable model output here was a repair-versus-replace recommendation, and it was only possible because a boring decision months earlier forced every fault to be tagged to an asset.

Reflection

What the system actually replaced.

Daftarkhwan did not have a communication problem. Fifteen group chats moved information around perfectly well. What they lacked was any residue — nothing that survived the conversation, accumulated against the equipment, or could be counted at the end of a month.

Most of the work was not building the ticketing system. It was noticing which fields the people using it were quietly refusing to fill in, and then taking those fields away.

Track accountability, not just completion.
