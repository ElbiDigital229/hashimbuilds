Giving Fifteen Buildings A Memory. Five Steps To Two.

Fifteen buildings and more than ten thousand pieces of equipment, run through fourteen WhatsApp threads — where information moved perfectly well and nothing accumulated against the thing that kept breaking.

The Problem

Fifteen buildings, and no operational memory.

Daftarkhwan runs coworking space across Pakistan: roughly fifteen branches, each a full building hosting three to four hundred members, more than five thousand members in total, and a team of around a hundred people. Underneath them sit well over ten thousand moving assets — air conditioners, generators, UPS units, internet links, lighting.

Coordination happened in WhatsApp. A thread per branch, separate threads for operations, fourteen or fifteen of them held in a manager’s head at once. The information moved. It just never settled anywhere: nothing accumulated against the asset, the branch, the owner, the repair, the blocker or the cost.

The organisation’s memory was living inside people and chat history.

Discovery

Every repair looked like the first one.

Because faults were reported conversationally, a unit that had been patched four times was indistinguishable from a unit being patched for the first time. Servicing slipped, small faults were left until they became failures, and the failures were expensive.

The cost did not stop at maintenance. A member whose room is too hot, whose internet drops, whose lift is out, experiences that as the product. Unresolved faults fed into churn, and churn into revenue.

Conversation produced four incidents. Asset history revealed one recurring problem.

What We Shipped First

We built the obvious thing: a ticketing system.

A member sees a problem, opens the app, photographs it, and fills in a form: title, description, priority, assignee, due date. The ticket routes to a department and lands in one queue with a status, an owner and an activity trail. Mobile apps went out so the people who walk the buildings were not tied to a desk.

Tickets arrived. Tickets got closed. It took a few weeks of real use to see that every field we had asked a human to fill in was carrying bad data.

The software worked. The assumptions did not.

Where It Broke

If everyone can choose urgent, everything becomes urgent.

Reporters set their own priority, so almost everything arrived as P1. Operations opened a queue in which nothing outranked anything else, which is the same as having no priority at all.

The other two fields failed just as quietly. Reporters frequently did not know who owned a given fault, so assignment fell back to operations. And every due date was set to today, because everyone wants their own problem fixed now.

A priority field everyone marks P1 in is not a priority system.

The Real Problem

We were asking reporters to run operations.

Priority

People benefit from calling their own issue urgent. Asking them to rank it against everyone else’s is asking them to act against their own interest.

Assignee

A member reporting a broken air conditioner has no idea which of a hundred staff owns it. Neither does most of the staff.

Due date

Today, every time. Not carelessness — it is the only honest answer to “when do you want this fixed?”

Underneath all three sat the same structural fault: tickets went to operations, who read them and passed them on. Two of the five steps between a fault and a fix were people moving information rather than repairing anything.

Every behaviour was rational. The product was asking the wrong questions.

Let The Asset Decide

Derive what the system already knows.

We deleted the priority field. We deleted the assignee field. Title and description collapsed into one box. What remained was the one thing a reporter genuinely knows and has no incentive to distort: which piece of equipment is broken, and what it is doing.

Priority comes from the asset

I sat with the operations team and we went through the register. Is a failed air conditioner a P1 or a P2? A generator? A lift? Once that mapping existed, priority stopped being a claim and became a property of the equipment.

Routing comes from the asset

The same tag drives department. Generator faults reach the genset team, cleanliness reaches housekeeping, HVAC reaches HVAC. The ticket arrives with the right people already on it.

The rules belong to the client

All of it is editable in an admin panel: if the asset matches this, assign that department, at that priority. This was the requirement I cared most about. A routing system that needs its builder every time the organisation changes shape is a dependency, not a tool.

The reporter’s job shrank to two things they cannot get wrong: what is broken, and what it looks like.

The Blocker System

Measure the delay, not just the person.

Once performance was visible, the reports started producing unfair answers. An engineer sitting at thirty per cent completion looked like the problem. He was not. He had inspected his tickets, priced the fix, and been waiting on finance to release funds that never came.

A low completion rate can mean poor performance. It can equally mean waiting on finance, procurement, a vendor, a part or building access. So assignees can mark a ticket blocked and tag whoever is holding it. The ticket stays open, the clock keeps running, and elapsed time attaches to the party that caused it.

Same metric, opposite management decision.

Where AI Belonged

First nothing, then titles, then the thing worth having.

The first version had no AI in it at all, and that was deliberate. Priority derivation, routing, SLA and escalation are ordinary deterministic logic, and they carried most of the benefit. Anything a rule can do predictably should not be handed to a model.

The first real use was small. Because the form had collapsed to one field, tickets had good descriptions and no titles — so a model writes the title from the description. The reporter gets one box; the queue still reads cleanly.

The rest arrived only once history existed. Recurring schedules began generating preventive maintenance against real equipment on a cadence, with checklists attached — servicing an air conditioner because its record says it is due, rather than because it has already failed.

The valuable capability was unlocked months earlier by a boring decision: attach every repair to an asset.

Repair vs Replace

History turned maintenance into an economic decision.

Two to three months in, the record could finally speak. One air conditioner had been repaired four times in two months and had cost a hundred and ten thousand rupees. Projected across a year, continuing to repair it costs roughly twice what replacing it would — and replacement carries a warranty that resets the risk.

It matters how that recommendation is produced. The arithmetic — repair frequency, accumulated spend, projected annual cost against replacement cost — is deterministic. A model can summarise the history and explain the reasoning. Neither one approves the money.

AI did not create the evidence. Structured asset history did.

The platform is live at ticket.truenorthpk.com, with an Android app wrapping the same system and push notifications to the people who walk the buildings.

Built as a single TypeScript codebase — React and Vite on the front, Express and Prisma against PostgreSQL behind it — running twenty-two server modules, thirty-three screens, sixty permission keys and nineteen notification event types, deployed on a self-hosted runner behind nginx.

What I’d Carry Forward

Four things this project taught me properly.

Never ask users to declare something they benefit from exaggerating.

Self-declared priority is not a data-quality problem you can train out of people. It is a design error, and the fix is to derive the field from something the reporter has no stake in.

A form people defeat is a form that asked the wrong questions.

Everything dumped in the title and every due date set to today told us precisely what was worth capturing and what was not. That behaviour was the research.

Measure the delay, not just the person.

Any completion metric without a blocking model will eventually punish the wrong people, and it will do it confidently.

Earn the AI by capturing the data first.

The valuable output here was a repair-versus-replace recommendation, and it was only possible because a boring decision months earlier forced every fault to be tagged to an asset.

Reflection

What the system actually replaced.

Daftarkhwan never had a communication problem. Fifteen threads moved information around perfectly well, every day, for years. What they lacked was residue — anything that survived the conversation, accumulated against the equipment, and could be counted at the end of a month.

Most of the work was not building a ticketing system. It was watching which fields the people using it were quietly refusing to fill in, and then taking those fields away.

The system did not replace WhatsApp. It replaced forgetting.
