Overview

I owned the product that sat between fintech ambition and banking reality.

VictorFi was Payments-as-a-Service and Banking-as-a-Service infrastructure. It sat between fintech products and the banks and rails underneath them — ACH, wires, RTP, FedNow — giving customers one way to send, receive and manage money across all of them.

My job was not to expose payment APIs. It was to decide what the product was: which concepts were shared across every institution, which genuinely had to differ, and what the team should therefore build. Almost everything below follows from that one question.

Problem

The problem I inherited wasn't payments. It was combinatorics.

Banks wanted fintech growth. Fintechs wanted bank access. Connecting them was the painful part, because every integration arrived with its own rails, bank rules, onboarding requirements, compliance controls, approval structures, reconciliation processes, edge cases, failure states, permissions and audit requirements.

This is the argument I had to make inside the company, and it is the one that shaped everything after it. The cost was not any single integration. It was that the cost multiplied — every new customer against every new rail — so growth and engineering load rose together. A sales win and an engineering crisis were the same event.

So the market did not have a payments problem. It had a complexity problem, and complexity is a product problem before it is an engineering one.

Making Sense of the Mess

My first job was to find the product hidden inside the complexity.

To a customer, a payment is one instruction: send $50,000. To the platform it is twelve decisions. Every one of them needed a defined owner, a state, a failure path and an audit record — and writing those definitions was my work, not engineering's.

My job was to turn this complexity into a product customers could understand without stripping away the controls banks depended on.

Three constituencies wanted three different products.

Discovery kept returning the same three positions, and they did not reconcile on their own. I could not average them — averaging would have produced a product that satisfied none. I had to find what was genuinely common underneath all three, and be willing to tell each of them what they were not getting.

Fintechs wanted speed

Give us one clean API and let us move money.
  • Faster integrations
  • Simpler APIs
  • Fewer operational blockers
  • Clearer workflows
  • Faster onboarding

What I could not give them: fewer controls.

Banks wanted control

We need approvals, limits, permissions and auditability.
  • Approvals and permissions
  • Limits
  • Auditability
  • Compliance visibility
  • Operational oversight
  • Reconciliation

What I could not give them: a bespoke platform.

Engineering needed consistency

We can't build a different product for every bank.
  • Stable product models
  • Reusable workflows
  • Predictable requirements
  • Fewer bespoke implementations
  • Clear edge-case handling

What I could not give them: a frozen spec.

The Decision

Two obvious answers, and I rejected both.

Option A — build each bank exactly what it asks for.

The easiest thing to say yes to, and the one commercial pressure pushes hardest toward. It wins the implementation in front of you.

I rejected it because the cost is deferred, not avoided. Every new customer introduces new logic, new approval structures, new workflows, more QA and more technical debt, and the product fragments into as many products as we have customers. The bill arrives two years later, on someone else's watch.

Option B — force every bank into one rigid workflow.

The opposite error, and the one engineering leans toward: cleaner architecture, simpler model, easier QA and documentation.

I rejected this one too. Banks genuinely differ in approval hierarchies, compliance requirements, limits, permissions and operational controls. A single fixed workflow is not a simplification of that reality, it is a denial of it, and the first serious institution to evaluate us would find the gap.

The call: standardize the core, configure the edges.

Rather than a bespoke product per institution, I identified the reusable product concepts sitting underneath the different implementations, and drew the line between what was shared and what was configurable. That line is the product decision. Everything downstream — engineering effort, onboarding time, QA surface, how fast sales could say yes — followed from where I put it.

Shared core — one of each, forever

  • Transaction model
  • Payment states
  • Permissions framework
  • Audit model
  • Reconciliation model
  • User roles
  • Common operator workflows

Configurable — per institution

  • Approvals
  • Limits
  • Rules
  • Permissions
  • Compliance requirements
  • Operational controls

Standardize what should be common. Configure what genuinely needs to differ.

This became one of the governing principles behind scaling the platform, and the sentence I used to refuse work. When a request arrived that would have meant new core logic for one customer, the question was no longer whether we could build it. It was whether it belonged in the core — and if it did not, it became configuration or it did not ship.

A smaller decision, the same way: the transaction list.

The operator view went through three explorations rather than straight to polished UI. The choice was between visibility and comprehension, and it mattered because two different users — an operations analyst and a fintech customer — were going to open the same screen.

A — Dense operator table

Maximum visibility, good for operations. High cognitive load and hard for less technical users.

B — Simplified fintech view

Easy to understand and cleaner. Hides too much operational detail; weak for investigation and support.

C — Hybrid operator view — chosen

Clear transaction state, enough operational detail, filtering and drill-down. The better balance of clarity and control.

Changing How We Shipped

The highest-leverage thing I did was not a feature.

Compliance was arriving as a checkpoint after build. When requirements surfaced that late, user flows were redesigned, engineering work was revisited, releases slowed, controls felt bolted on, and customers met friction that existed for no reason they could see.

I moved compliance into product definition. Not a review gate earlier in the same process — an actual participant in deciding what the product was, before a flow was drawn.

That single change altered what the team built. Permissions, approvals, auditability and operational controls stopped being requirements imposed on a design and became part of the intended product experience from the start. It is the change I would point to first, because it improved every subsequent release rather than one feature.

Controls became product behavior, not late-stage patches.

Scaling Without Rebuilding

I made onboarding a configuration problem, not an engineering one.

Every institution arrived with different internal processes, documentation, approval models, risk controls, permissions and operational ownership. If each of those required engineering, the core decision would have leaked away one exception at a time. So the test I applied to onboarding was simple: could a new institution go live without a single line of core code changing?

Implementation-led

Requirements gathered through implementation and account teams — the entry point, not the mechanism.

Guided configuration

Admins configure roles, approval rules, limits, permissions and operational controls themselves.

Core product

Ongoing management lives inside VictorFi once implementation is complete, with no bespoke surface to maintain.

Different institutions could have different requirements without creating different products.

What Shipped

One mental model, across rails that behave nothing alike.

The goal was never to pretend ACH, wire and RTP are the same. It was to give a user one consistent way to think about a payment while the rail-specific complexity stayed underneath, where it belonged.

The detail I care most about is the bottom of that diagram. Success, failure and return all terminate in the same reconciliation and audit trail. That was a deliberate specification, not a convenience — it is the property that let a bank's risk function accept the platform, and it came out of the compliance decision two sections earlier.

How I Worked

I sat where the ambiguity was.

Requirements arrived from customers, banks and go-to-market simultaneously, in different vocabularies, often contradicting each other. They left as one decision engineering, compliance, design and QA could act on. That translation was the job — not producing screens.

How the method evolved

The loop stayed the same for five years. What changed was how much of it I could explore before the first conversation.

2020–2022 · Manual

Research → Synthesize → Write PRDs → User flows → QA scenarios → Handoff
  • Manual discovery synthesis
  • Manual requirements drafting
  • Edge cases surfaced mainly through reviews
  • QA scenarios manually created
  • Documentation manually structured

2023–2025 · AI-assisted

Research → AI synthesis → Edge cases → QA scenarios → Draft PRDs → Refine & decide (human) → Handoff
  • Conversation and research synthesis
  • Requirement interrogation
  • Edge-case generation
  • Acceptance criteria
  • QA scenario generation
  • Documentation and product exploration

What AI helps with

  • Summarizing customer feedback
  • Drafting requirements and flows
  • Exploring edge cases
  • Generating QA scenarios
  • Comparing approaches

What still requires me

  • Product judgment and trade-offs
  • Compliance interpretation
  • Prioritization and sequencing
  • Customer empathy

AI improved speed and exploration. It did not replace product judgment.

Compliance interpretation, prioritisation, customer judgment, risk decisions and final requirements stayed human-led. The real change was arriving at engineering, customer and compliance conversations with more scenarios already explored — which meant the conversations were about deciding, not discovering.

Reflection

What I learned

The simplest fintech experiences hide enormous complexity.

My job was not to remove the complexity. It was to decide which complexity customers needed to see and which belonged inside the platform — and to be able to defend that line to a fintech, a bank and an engineer in three different conversations on the same day.

Product ownership in fintech is mostly about trade-offs.

Speed, compliance, customer needs, technical scalability and operational reliability are permanently in tension. The job is to make those trade-offs deliberately rather than accidentally, and to write down why, so the decision survives the person who made it.

The most valuable decisions were the ones that let us say no.

Standardize the core, configure the edges was not only an architecture. It was a rule that let the whole company refuse the wrong work without arguing about it each time.

Enterprise fintech isn't about removing complexity. It's about placing complexity in the right layer so customers can move fast without sacrificing trust, visibility, or control.
