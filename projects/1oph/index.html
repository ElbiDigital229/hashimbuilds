The Physical Wall

The catalogue could not be photographed the old way.

1-OPH makes high-value sculpture and fountains for villas, hotels, courtyards and public space in the Gulf. Pieces commonly sell from around $30,000, and fabrication alone is a serious share of that.

A buyer at that price is not evaluating an object on a white cutout. They need scale against a real room, architectural fit, how the material behaves in light, several angles, day against night, and detail close enough to judge finish. Roughly seven views, per product, across a catalogue of more than a thousand — over seven thousand finished images.

Producing that physically means fabricate, transport, install, secure a premium location, style it, crew it, light it, shoot it, retouch it — then repeat. The problem was never that photography was impossible. It was that repeating it at catalogue scale would take years and cost millions.

The economics made AI worth trying. The pilot showed why AI alone was not enough.

The Pilot

Ten products looked good. Then we looked closer.

We started with about ten products rather than a plan. The client supplies a sketch or a source image; the system has to understand the product, design an environment, place it credibly, hold the product’s identity and hold the room’s identity across a coordinated set.

The models could already make attractive pictures. Closer review exposed four failure modes instead — two about judgement, two about consistency. The environments did not match the client’s taste, placement was architecturally wrong, the sculpture drifted between renders, and seven views of “the same room” were visibly seven different rooms.

Pretty is not reliable. That became the actual product problem.

The first failures were not rendering problems. They were failures of taste and context — so taste had to stop being an opinion.

The Taste System

Stop asking for “luxury.” Define what luxury means.

“Luxury” is far too vague to use as a production instruction. I asked the client for environments he approved, environments he rejected, and placement references per product category, then decomposed them into things a system can act on: lighting, greenery, product dominance, geography, material palette and furniture density.

That preference memory feeds an interior design agent whose only job is to produce a structured brief — product, room, placement, lighting, camera — before anything expensive renders. The room is derived partly from the object standing in it: its material, dominant colour, finish and visual weight.

Once we could design the right room, we had to make sure both the product and the room stayed that way.

Make It Consistent

Pretty became optional. Fidelity became contractual.

A beautiful image is still unusable if the sculpture’s geometry, base, material or scale changed. The same is true of the environment: seven attractive images are worthless if they look like seven different rooms. Both became hard production requirements rather than hopes.

Room drift was never going to be fixed by better prompting, because each shot was an independent roll of the dice. So the approved environment became a canonical scene state — a locked object every later view is anchored to.

Around the model sits a harness that owns context, product metadata, retrieval, preference memory, guardrails, retries, evals and thresholds, the canonical scene, escalation, cost and publishing. Guardrails enforce product geometry and the plinth, cultural appropriateness, Gulf-appropriate geography, no unnecessary water, no invented product details, and no repeated room styles across the catalogue.

A generative model is not a product. The thing around it is the product.

Reliability solved one problem. At scale, the next question became cost.

Make It Economical

Reject early. Escalate late. Human attention is expensive.

Ten products worked. A hundred exposed the real economics — about seven hundred images, and in an early automated run roughly half the concepts were rejected after full generation. That is around three hundred and fifty finished images with no commercial value.

So two evals went in front of the expensive step. A concept eval scores the brief before anything renders. Then exactly one image is generated and a visual eval scores that. The remaining six are produced only if the proof passes.

Approval improved, but reviewing every first image created a new bottleneck — the client became a mandatory checkpoint on every product. The fix was confidence routing: the proof is scored out of ten, seven or above continues automatically, below seven goes to a person. On a five-hundred product batch that meant roughly fifty items reaching a human.

Humans are a scarce capability the system routes to, not a checkpoint every job queues behind.

Once quality and human effort were both routable, the workflow was ready to become a production system.

The Production System

By the end, generation was one step in a much larger operating model.

Product data and previous approvals are retrieved first. The interior design agent plans the environment. Concept evals filter bad directions. One proof is generated and scored by a multimodal evaluator. Confidence decides whether the system continues or escalates. Approved scenes are locked before the full shot family is produced, then final QA and publishing close the loop.

What began as an experiment with generative imagery became an operating model for an AI photography and interior-design team.

What I’d Carry Forward

Five things this system taught me.

Visual quality is not production reliability.

A model that produces a beautiful image eight times in ten is a great demo and an unusable pipeline. Consistency across a set is a different property from quality within one frame, and it is the one a catalogue needs.

Put the eval where the cost starts, not where it ends.

Everything expensive in this system happened after a decision that could have been scored for almost nothing. Moving judgement in front of generation was worth more than any improvement to the generation itself.

Taste is a requirement. Write it down.

“He’ll know it when he sees it” is not a constraint a system can satisfy. Decomposing preference into lighting, placement, language, geography and palette made it executable and reviewable.

Lock the state you have already paid for.

The canonical scene exists because an approved room is an asset. Regenerating it seven times throws away the one thing that already cleared review.

Route to humans. Do not queue behind them.

The client was the highest-value and scarcest reviewer in the process. Treating his attention as something to spend selectively is what let the throughput move.

Reflection

What was actually built.

The deliverable looks like images. It is not. It is a production system with a generative model somewhere inside it — wrapped in retrieval, preference memory, guardrails, two evaluation gates, a confidence router, a locked scene state and a publishing step.

Almost none of the difficulty was in the model. It was in deciding what to check, when to check it, what to do when a check failed, and whose time a failure was allowed to cost.

The model was one step. Everything around it was the product.
